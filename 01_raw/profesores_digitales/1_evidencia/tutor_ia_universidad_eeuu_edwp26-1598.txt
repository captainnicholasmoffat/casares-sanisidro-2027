The Effects of Course-Integrated AI Tutoring 
on Student Performance and Engagement:
A Randomized University Trial
We evaluate access to a course-integrated generative AI (GenAI) tutor in a randomized controlled trial 
involving 2,379 undergraduate students and 30 instructors across multiple disciplines in a large U.S. public 
university. Among sections of the same course, tutor access reduced final grades by 0.37 standard 
deviations and learning management system participation by 0.90 standard deviations. Participation 
declines were similarly large in the full sample, and estimated academic losses were greater among 
first-generation students. These findings suggest that introducing an AI tutor may displace engagement 
with existing learning activities without improving academic performance, with potentially differential 
impact across student groups.

Abstract
We evaluate access to a course-integrated generative AI (GenAI) tutor in a random-
ized controlled trial involving 2,379 undergraduate students and 30 instructors across
multiple disciplines in a large U.S. public university. Among sections of the same
course, tutor access reduced final grades by 0.37 standard deviations and learning
management system participation by 0.90 standard deviations. Participation declines
were similarly large in the full sample, and estimated academic losses were greater
among first-generation students. These findings suggest that introducing an AI tutor
may displace engagement with existing learning activities without improving aca-
demic performance, with potentially differential impact across student groups.
JEL Codes: I23, I21, C93, O33.
Keywords: Generative AI; Higher Education; AI Tutoring; Randomized Controlled
Trial; Academic Performance; Student Engagement.

Introduction
Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is reshaping human productivity—not only by
changing how people perform tasks, but also by altering the learning procedures through
which they acquire the knowledge and skills to perform them (Deng et al., 2025; Kasneci
et al., 2023; McGrath et al., 2025; Purwono et al., 2025). In education, GenAI offers the
prospect of personalized instructional support at unprecedented scale (Farrokhnia et al.,
2024; Grassini, 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023), while also raising a central challenge: how to
design and deploy GenAI-powered tools that enhance rather than replace human learn-
ing. Although intelligent tutoring systems have a long history in educational research
and practice, the widespread availability of large language model (LLM)–based conver-
sational tools since late 2022 has accelerated efforts to integrate AI into everyday learning
environments (Winkler and Söllner, 2018; Xu et al., 2024; Al-Abdullatif et al., 2023). By
providing on-demand explanations, feedback, and practice tailored to students’ needs
and learning stages, these tools could expand access to individualized tutoring and im-
prove learning outcomes (Michel-Villarreal et al., 2023; Rudolph et al., 2023).
However, focusing on efficiency of performance with AI assistance is not the same
as developing the knowledge and skills needed to perform independently. AI may sup-
port the cognitive work through which students develop understanding, or potentially
serve as a complete substitute for it.
Recent studies illustrate this tension: access to
general-purpose AI can improve performance on assisted tasks while undermining sub-
sequent independent performance—the so-called “learning penalty”—although these ad-
verse effects may be mitigated by purpose-built tutoring safeguards (Strömberg et al.,
2026; Bastani et al., 2025). AI assistance can reduce students’ mental effort or cognitive
load (Avello-Martínez et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024), potentially easing
barriers to understanding. Yet these reductions may come at a cost: Stadler et al. (2024)
found that LLM assistance reduced mental effort while compromising the depth of scien-
tific inquiry. An EEG-based essay-writing study likewise documented differences in neu-
1

ral engagement across AI-assisted and independent writing conditions (Kosmyna et al.,
2025). Whether embedding GenAI in routine educational settings strengthens learning
or displaces the effort and engagement that sustain it therefore remains a consequential
empirical question.
In higher education, the adoption of GenAI has outpaced evidence on its educational
consequences, raising concerns about academic integrity and equitable access to learning
support (Deng et al., 2025; Purwono et al., 2025; Walczak and Cellary, 2023; Cotton et al.,
2024). Beyond facilitating academic misconduct, GenAI may reshape how students en-
gage with the broader learning environment. Its availability could change when students
study, whether they consult primary course materials, and how often they seek assis-
tance from instructors or peers. These behavioral consequences remain underexplored,
yet they may be central to understanding whether AI complements or displaces existing
learning activities. Moreover, unequal access to AI tools and differences in how students
use them may reinforce educational inequalities (Yu et al., 2024). Together, these concerns
underscore the need for systematic evidence on both academic and behavioral outcomes
to guide institutional policy and the responsible adoption of GenAI in higher education.
The current study presents findings from, to our knowledge, the first large-scale ran-
domized experiment evaluating the effects of AI tutoring on student learning at a large
U.S. public university. Our research setting and design offer several strengths. First,
the study includes approximately 2,400 undergraduate students and 30 instructors across
courses spanning STEM, the social sciences, the humanities, and education. This disci-
plinary breadth, together with students’ diverse demographics and socioeconomic back-
grounds, strengthens the potential generalizability of our findings. Second, we random-
ized instructors within blocks of identical or similar courses, allowing comparisons within
broadly comparable instructional settings. We report estimates for the full sample and
an exact-match subsample restricted to blocks containing sections of the same course.
Third, the AI tutor we examine is a GPT-4o–based virtual study assistant (VSA) that uses
2

retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to draw on instructor-selected course materials
within the institution’s Enterprise Learning Management System (ELMS). Instructors se-
lect the course materials made available to the tutor, choose between direct instruction
and tutoring modes, and can adjust their instruction based on the types of prompts or
questions students are generating when using the VSA. This instructor-guided config-
uration and course site integration allow us to evaluate AI tutoring within an applied
routine learning environment rather than in a stand-alone experimental setting. Finally,
alongside course performance, we collect rich data on students’ online course activity and
interactions with the AI tutor, as well as survey responses from both students and instruc-
tors, allowing us to investigate potential mechanisms underlying the observed effects.
Conducted during the fall semester of the 2025–26 academic year, our study yields
several important findings. We found that access to the AI tutor negatively affected stu-
dent final course grades by 0.27–0.37 standard deviations (SDs), with the clearest evidence
in the exact-match sample. Although several studies have found that GenAI improves
short-term performance (Bastani et al., 2025; Strömberg et al., 2026), our estimated effects
on homework scores are null or negative.
Alongside the adverse academic estimates, we found substantial reductions in stu-
dents’ engagement with the existing course platform. In the exact-match sample, AI tutor
access reduced course page views (−0.37 SDs), active days (−0.51 SDs), and recorded par-
ticipation in instructor-designed online activities (−0.90 SDs), with similar reductions in
the full sample. Some substitution is expected given the tool’s intended functions—for
example, students may ask the tutor about course logistics rather than consult the course
page.
However, the decline in recorded participation, which includes discussion re-
sponses, assignment submissions, and quizzes, raises concerns about substitution beyond
information retrieval. Survey responses provided complementary descriptive evidence:
compared with nonusers in treatment classrooms, AI tutor users were more likely to re-
port asking instructors fewer questions about course content and sending the instructor
3

fewer emails or ELMS messages, potentially changing the way in which these learners
are engaged in the learning process.
We also found differences across student groups in both AI tutor use and its effects.
Hispanic students were more likely to use the AI tutor than their White peers and stu-
dents in higher high school GPA quartiles (among study participants) generally had lower
estimated uptake than those in the bottom quartile, although differences were not uni-
formly significant. Evidence of heterogeneity in the effects on learning and behavior was
generally limited, however, the estimated final-grade losses among first-generation stu-
dents were more than twice those among continuing-generation students in both samples,
raising important concerns about potentially unequal impact of deploying AI tools.
Analysis of student requests provided additional descriptive evidence about how stu-
dents used the tutor. Approximately 73.8% of requests sought information, explanations,
or solutions, compared with 11.3% involving practice and test preparation and 0.7% seek-
ing feedback on students’ own work. While these categories do not directly provide ev-
idence on cognitive offloading, the patterns we observe suggest that students predomi-
nantly used the VSA for passive, on-demand assistance rather than actively seeking eval-
uation of their own attempts.
These findings should be interpreted in light of the institutional and implementation
context. Students in both conditions had free access to other university-supported AI
tools, including ChatGPT and Gemini. Given that only approximately 15% of treated stu-
dents used the VSA at least once, it is possible that instructors introducing and encourag-
ing use of the course-specific tutor may have changed GenAI use beyond the VSA itself.
Instructors’ and researchers’ ability to view student–tutor interactions may also have in-
fluenced what students were willing to ask, limiting comparability with privately used,
stand-alone tools. Finally, few instructors switched from the default direct-instruction
mode to the tutor mode, which guides students toward a correct response, rather than
providing them with a correct answer immediately (only two instructors ended the term
4

with this mode in place). The findings reported here thus concern the VSA as configured
and introduced in this study, rather than establishing the effects of more scaffolded AI
tutoring.
Our study builds on the growing literature on GenAI in education, particularly the
still-limited experimental evidence from higher education.1 In K–12 settings, Sungu et al.
(2026) find that instructor access to GenAI reduced students’ intrinsic motivation without
improving exam scores overall, with adverse academic effects among students of initially
lower-performing instructors. Bastani et al. (2025) show that general-purpose AI access
impaired subsequent independent performance in high school mathematics, whereas tu-
toring guardrails mitigated this harm. Using a difference-in-differences design and draw-
ing on survey data, Strömberg et al. (2026) similarly find that GenAI adoption in high
school reduced exam performance despite initially improving homework scores.
Recent experiments emphasize the importance of engagement and instructional struc-
ture. Oreopoulos and Low (2026) find modest achievement gains from Khan Academy
combined with Khanmigo, but limited sustained tutor use. However, their design does
not isolate AI’s incremental contribution. Comparing AI support with the same practice
platform without AI, Oreopoulos et al. (2026) find that the AI-enhanced platform leads
to slower progression. They also find suggestive evidence of improved recovery from
mistakes, and modest, marginally significant delayed-learning gains on one practice ex-
ercise under mastery-based progression. Together, these studies indicate that GenAI’s
educational effects depend on tutor configuration, instructional scaffolding, and realized
engagement, and that improvements during assisted practice may not necessarily trans-
late into independent learning.
In higher education, Barcaui (2025) uses an RCT to examine the impact of GenAI on
undergraduates learning AI. The findings closely align with those from Bastani et al.
(2025) and Strömberg et al. (2026)—unrestricted use of ChatGPT contributes to cognitive-

offloading, reduces student effort, and negatively impacts student knowledge retention.
In contrast, Kestin et al. (2025) compare a customized AI tutor with an in-class active
learning lesson in an undergraduate physics course at Harvard University. Students as-
signed to the tutor achieved substantially larger immediate learning gains in less time
and reported greater engagement and motivation. These findings demonstrate the po-
tential of purpose-built AI tutoring. However, its applicability beyond a single course at
a highly selective university remains uncertain. The distinct findings from these two stud-
ies also point to the importance of evaluating purpose-built AI and general purpose AI
learning tools separately. Lastly, Thoeni and Fryer (2026) evaluate a RAG-based chatbot
in a semester-long RCT involving 500 students in a marketing course and find no sta-
tistically significant effects on achievement, interest, self-efficacy, or engagement. Their
results suggest that grounding AI assistance in course-specific materials alone may be
insufficient to improve educational outcomes.
Although informative, these studies leave several important questions unresolved.
First, the higher-education trials examine individual courses, leaving limited evidence
across disciplines and student populations. Second, student-level randomization within
classrooms creates opportunities for spillovers that may narrow treatment–control con-
trasts, and our instructor-level assignment reduces this particular risk. Third, the multi-
instructor experiment by Sungu et al. (2026) evaluates instructor-facing GenAI rather
than student-facing assistance. Our study addresses these gaps by examining course-
integrated AI tutor access across disciplines, with particular attention to how its intro-
duction changes students’ engagement with existing course activities alongside academic
performance.

Study Context
The current investigation was conducted at the University of Maryland (UMD), a public,
four-year university in the Mid-Atlantic United States with approximately 40,000 stu-
dents (30,000 undergraduate, 10,000 graduate) and 4,500 faculty across 12 colleges rep-
resenting distinct disciplines. The university’s Division of Information Technology pro-
visions a number of custom and licensed generative AI tools, including a VSA chatbot,
the utility of which is the focus of the current study. The VSA was developed and made
available to select instructors on a preliminary basis beginning in 2024, with the goal of
piloting emerging GenAI technology to interested faculty within an instructional course
space. After an academic year of limited pilot uses, the current study was motivated by
the need to evaluate the VSA’s utility and impacts on learning outcomes more broadly
prior to considering campus-wide deployment of the tool within the learning manage-
ment system. This research was approved by UMD’s Institutional Review Board (proto-
col number K-2768).
2.1
Virtual Study Assistant
The VSA provides a GPT chatbot (GPT 4.o) with RAG technology for use in course spaces
within the institution’s ELMS, which hosts the online space for each course at UMD. The
VSA’s RAG architecture allows it to primarily draw content directly from each course’s
online materials, including pages, PDFs, text documents, Powerpoint slides, and video
transcripts. By drawing on these materials and providing citations for the materials it
draws in its responses, the tool is intended to improve the relevance and accuracy of
its answers while allowing students to consult the underlying sources. However, it is
worth noting that the VSA does use an underlying pre-trained LLM. As such, it is sub-
ject to the same limitations of other LLMs (e.g., hallucinations), and while responses are
designed to remain grounded in course materials, the VSA may use its pre-trained gen-
7

erative capabilities to create supporting content that is not sourced from course materials
(e.g., generating incorrect answer choices for practice quizzes or generating hints in tu-
tor mode). As the administrator of their VSA, the instructor can control what categories
of learning materials and what specific sources are pulled from the course space. When
source content is added, removed, or modified, these changes are detected, and the VSA
initiates a nightly ingest process where the source information is scanned and processed
into a vector database to enable the RAG technology. The technological architecture of
the VSA is visualized in Figure A1.
Once activated, the VSA appears as an integrated tool in the sidebar navigation menu
of the digital course space. Students can interact with the VSA chatbot by querying it.
The VSA restricts its responses to queries that it deems relevant to course content. For
example, if a student asks an English literature question to the VSA in a biology class, the
VSA would indicate to the student the question is out of scope. The VSA also provides
citations if the instructor makes that content public. When a video lecture is cited, a link
is provided to the timestamp in the video that has the answer. Figure A2 shows several
screenshots of student views of the VSA. Students can provide feedback using a thumbs
up or thumbs down response, along with comments that are provided to the instructor.
Instructors are able to view students’ anonymized prompts and VSA responses in an
administrative console, as well as aggregated data related to the frequency and content
of student prompts (see Figure A3). Additionally, instructors can freely switch between
a tutor mode, which guides students toward an answer, and a direct instruction mode,
which provides more explicit explanations.
2.2
Experimental Design
The current study took place during the Fall 2025 semester. Instructors of record were
recruited beginning prior to the semester and continuing through week 7 of the 16-week
semester. Instructors provided their informed consent to participate. We made every
8

effort to recruit instructors teaching courses from a variety of disciplines and academic
levels. Instructors were admitted to the study only if they used a distinct ELMS course
page for their section(s), and they indicated that a majority of their course content (e.g.,
syllabus, readings, videos, and/or assignments) would be posted to that course page, as
the VSA requires these materials to function as intended. All students in participating
courses were notified and enrolled into the study unless they chose to opt out or were
under 18 at study onset. A full timeline of the study rollout is presented in Figure A4.
The study design was a group-randomized trial, so randomization occurred at the
instructor level within course blocks. Instructors were first grouped based on teaching the
same or similar courses, with randomized assignment to the treatment condition (VSA
on) or control condition (VSA off) then occurring within each group, such that courses
from each group were represented in both conditions. All students taught by an instructor
were assigned to the instructor’s condition for that course. Thus, if the instructor was
assigned to the treatment condition, all of their students for the participating course had
access to the VSA, and if an instructor was assigned to the control condition, none of their
students had access to the VSA.
The instructor groupings occurred as follows: (a) If two or more instructors taught
different sections of the same course (i.e., an exact match), they were put into the same
group. Otherwise, groupings were made based on (b) instructors teaching courses in the
same level and program of study (e.g., two 300-level English courses), or else, (c) teaching
qualitatively similar courses based on an evaluation of course description, format, and
student number. For groups of two courses, one instructor was randomly assigned to the
treatment condition, and the other to the control condition; for groups of three, two were
assigned to treatment, and one to control. In order to maximize sample size, recruitment
continued after the semester started. While we attempted to enroll any later enrolled
instructors together as groups (according to the criteria described above), there were cases
in which a newly enrolled course better fit with an existing group. In these cases, the
9

instructor was added to the existing group and randomly assigned to the treatment or
control condition, regardless of existing assignments within the group.
Table A1 shows the distribution of courses by 13 randomization blocks. Participating
courses covered a range of disciplines, including biology, computer science, communi-
cation, English, family science, education, information studies, public policy, and math-
ematics. These courses represent 11 of the university’s 12 schools or colleges. Course
numbers ranged from the 100 to the 400 level, indicating representation from both lower-
and upper-division coursework. Nine blocks comprised sections of the same course (i.e.,
exact-match), while the remaining four blocks included 12 sections from different courses.
The combination of the nine exact-match blocks and the remaining four blocks comprises
the study’s full sample.
2.3
Study Conditions
After an instructor’s assignment to the treatment condition, the VSA was integrated as a
visible hyperlink within their ELMS course space, and instructors received an email from
the research team with instructions for setting up and activating the VSA. If instructors
did not activate the VSA, it was unavailable for their students. As our experiment aims
to evaluate how VSA access affects student outcomes rather than instructors’ uptake, we
removed instructors who did not activate the VSA from our sample (n = 3 instructors).
In addition to these initial instructions, instructors in the treatment condition received
links to customized how-to articles and video tutorials, as well as optional sample talking
points, ELMS announcement language, and syllabus text for sharing information about
the VSA with their students. Instructors also received automatic weekly insights emails
summarizing student use in their course (e.g., number and general topics of interactions).
Treated students received links to existing tutorials for using the tool, including a student-
targeted video and guide for using the tool. Beyond suggesting to instructors that they
introduce and demonstrate how to use the VSA with their students, the researchers pro-
10

vided no additional guidance for how they should incorporate the tool. No intervention
occurred in courses in the control condition; instructors were informed of their assign-
ment and were only provided sample talking points and announcement language for
discussing the study.

3.2
Randomization Balance Checks
The randomization procedure resulted in a balance of course and instructor character-
istics (e.g., enrollment, instructor age/race/rank) between the two conditions. Student
demographic characteristics (e.g., race, high school GPA) showed slightly more imbal-
ance, likely from student sorting into different instructors. Thus, our preferred models
include all demographic variables as controls in the subsequent regression analysis.
Tables 1 and A2 report balance for the two samples. Student characteristics are broadly
similar across conditions, although some differences emerge: in the full sample, treated
students are more likely to be White and less likely to be Asian, and have slightly lower
high school GPAs; in the exact-match sample, they differ in first-generation and inter-
national status. This pattern is consistent with student sorting across instructors rather
than a failure of randomization, since assignment occurred at the instructor level. Course
and instructor characteristics are comparable in the full sample, with the exception of
instructor race. Differences on instructor ranks are also sizable, although they are not
statistically significant. In the exact-match sample, treated instructors are slightly older
and assign fewer homework points; at the same time, they are more balanced regarding
race and academic ranks. We therefore include student demographics as controls in all
specifications and condition on randomization block throughout.

3.3
Outcome variables
Academic outcomes. Primary academic outcomes include final grades, which are posted
by the instructor on the ELMS course page and use a 0–100 scale, and course letter grade
of record converted to the GPA scale. The two measures may diverge both because of
their differing scales and because some instructors apply different conversion rubrics. A
secondary academic outcome is homework score, which is converted using points earned
over points available for assignments in the course space. Note that the researchers did
not have visibility into which assignments or exams may have taken place in person,
online with a test proctoring service, or online without proctoring, nor did the researchers
have insights into instructors’ policies around AI use for a given assignment.
ELMS engagement. The following measures related to ELMS activity were extracted
from the ELMS system: page views (i.e., the total number of times a student viewed a
page in ELMS during the semester), active days (i.e., the number of days in the semester
that the student had either a page view or participation in ELMS), and total participation,
which is the sum of all participated activities, including things such as submitting an
assignment, responding to a discussion, or taking a quiz. Total participation depends on
the activities required by the instructor in ELMS.
3.4
Survey Data
Students and instructors in both conditions received a survey at the end of the semester.
The survey assessed students’ general use of GenAI across the semester, perceptions of
student-instructor interactions (e.g., student questions for instructors; office hours atten-
dance), course and instructor evaluations, and for students in the treatment condition
who used the VSA, perceptions of the tool’s usefulness, accuracy, and usability. Survey
items were predominantly sourced from two prior surveys administered at the univer-
sity: a campus-wide survey on GenAI in teaching and learning, originally administered
13

in the 2023-2024 academic year, and an operational survey administered to seven courses
that piloted the VSA as part of a usability study in Summer 2025.
The survey was administered via the Qualtrics platform and distributed via invita-
tions sent through the Qualtrics mailer, which automatically records respondents’ email
addresses, enabling the researchers to link survey responses to other study data sources.
The survey occurred simultaneously for all participants, beginning on the second to last
week of classes (week 14 of the semester) and remaining open through finals (week 16).
Student nonrespondents received up to three reminder emails and two direct reminders
which appeared as one-time pop-up messages in ELMS within the participating course
page. Instructor nonrespondents received one reminder email per survey. Ten student
respondents were randomly selected to win $50, and four instructors were randomly se-
lected to win $500. We achieved response rates of 18% for students and 67% for instruc-
tors. We ultimately achieved response rates of 18% for students and 70% for instructors.
3.5
Patterns of VSA Usage: Students
We characterize student VSA usage using three measures: the number of interactions,
defined as user-generated questions or instructions submitted to the VSA; the number
of sessions, each of which may include multiple student prompts and VSA-generated
responses; and the number of unique users, defined as distinct students who use the VSA
in a given course during the relevant reporting period. A session begins when a student
opens the VSA and ends when the browser is closed or after two hours of user inactivity.
Based on Table 2, only 14.63% of treated students ever used the VSA. Figure 1 shows
that VSA use was uneven across students, over time, and across courses. Conditional
on using the tool at least once, approximately 40% of students had only one session, and
most had only a few sessions. The distribution nevertheless has a long right tail, with a
small number of students recording more than 20 sessions and some exceeding 40.
Usage also varied over the semester. Total interactions were generally higher in the
14

later instructional weeks, with pronounced peaks in weeks 10–11 and week 14, and a
decline during Thanksgiving week. The daily pattern for one large introductory STEM
course (bottom left panel) provides more direct evidence of assessment-related use: the
number of unique users increased around exam dates and was typically low between
exams. These patterns are consistent with students using the VSA episodically to support
exam preparation rather than as a regular study companion, although usage timing alone
does not establish the purpose of students’ interactions.
Finally, average VSA usage varied substantially across courses. The two heaviest-use
courses averaged roughly nine and just over three interactions per student, respectively;
both are large, foundational STEM courses taken mainly by first-year students. All other
courses averaged fewer than two interactions per student. These differences may reflect
variation in the VSA’s usefulness for particular course content and designs, the extent of
instructor encouragement, and students’ needs and preferences.
3.6
Patterns of VSA Usage: Instructors
We have limited data on how instructors used insights from the VSA to adjust their in-
struction. Among survey respondents in the treatment group (n = 10), 30% agreed that
they used information from emails about students’ VSA use to identify topics for review,
and 70% reported monitoring students’ questions and answers with the VSA at least
somewhat. Although all respondents reported mentioning the VSA to their students,
only 40% demonstrated how to use it, and none incorporated it into tasks or assignments.
Overall, these findings suggest that instructors engaged with the VSA to some extent
but did little to integrate it into their instruction, which may have contributed to the low
student engagement described above.

Equation (1) separately for the full and exact-match samples described above, and cluster
standard errors at the course-section level.
As our outcome variables are at different scales (e.g., course letter grade at a 0-4 scale,
grades at a 0-100 scale, page counts on a numeric scale, etc), we use and report the raw
scales in the analysis first to ease interpretation, and also convert the estimated coeffi-
cients using standard deviations among control students to show magnitudes and make
the effects comparable across outcomes.

Results
Our primary analysis uses all participating classrooms and accounts for the blocked ran-
domization design. We also report estimates for an exact-match subsample restricted
to randomization blocks comprising sections of the same course. This analysis assesses
whether the findings persist in more comparable instructional settings, although the re-
striction also changes the composition of the sample. We use the terms full sample and
exact-match sample to differentiate the two analyses.
5.1
VSA Uptake
Overall, approximately 15 percent of the students in treated classrooms used the VSA
at least once during the study. Figure 4 presents a complier analysis in the full sample
showing which student subgroups were more likely to use the VSA compared to their
counterparts. Within-classroom comparisons suggest that VSA uptake varied modestly
17

across student characteristics. Hispanic students were more likely to use the VSA than
White students, with an estimated difference of approximately 10 percentage points in
both the full and exact-match samples; this association is more precisely estimated in the
exact-match sample. Students in the second bottom quartile had higher uptake than stu-
dents in the bottom quartile, but higher high school GPA quartiles were not significantly
different in their uptake (note that quartiles here refer to the study population, and not
students’ GPA quartiles relative to their high school class). In the exact-match sample,
there is not any evidence of differential VSA usage by student demographics, including
gender, first-generation status, year, high school GPA quartile, or race (Figure A5).
5.2
Impact of VSA Access
Figures 2 and 3 compare the distributions of learning and behavioral outcomes across
treatment and control students in the full and exact-match samples, respectively. In the
full sample, final grades are less concentrated near the upper end of the distribution
among treatment students, with more density at lower grades. By contrast, the course
letter grade and homework-score distributions largely overlap. Differences in academic
outcomes are more visible in the exact-match sample, where treatment students have less
concentrated distributions near the highest grades, letter grade, and homework scores.
The figures show more consistent differences in ELMS engagement. In both samples,
the treatment distributions for page views and active days are shifted toward lower val-
ues. The contrast is particularly pronounced for total participation: treatment students’
activity is concentrated at relatively low levels, whereas the control distribution has sub-
stantially more mass at higher participation levels. The patterns suggest that VSA access
was associated with lower engagement with the existing learning management system.
Table 3 reports the corresponding ITT estimates (Equation 1), which capture the im-
pact of being assigned to having VSA access. We present the treatment effect estimates
without controls in the first row and with controls in the second row. Note that the full

sample results are shown in Panel A, and the exact-match results are shown in Panel B.5
In the full sample, there is not significant evidence of adverse treatment effects, likely
due to the lack of alignment between courses and grading. In the exact-match sample,
there are adverse treatment effects in two of the three achievement variables. Notably,
in the model that included control variables, course grades were 4.12 points lower in the
treatment condition, which is equivalent to 0.37 standard deviations. Similarly, course
letter grades in GPA scale were lower by 0.11 points (0.13 SDs). There is not evidence that
VSA access impacted homework scores.
Several aspects of these findings merit emphasis. First, final grades provide a more
granular, and arguably more objective, measure of course performance than course letter
grade and may therefore capture changes that do not translate into differences in letter
grade, particularly when the latter reflects discrete letter-grade thresholds. Second, the
large negative effect size (0.37) in the exact-match sample provides evidence that VSA
access adversely affected course performance, which is consistent with several recent re-
search studies that find adverse impacts of AI on student learning in K-12 settings (Bastani
et al., 2025; Strömberg et al., 2026), especially for performance on exams or high-stakes
testing contexts where AI is not available. Third, the estimated decline in final grade is
notable given that only approximately 15% of students who were offered access to the
VSA used it, and users averaged approximately four sessions. This contrast raises ques-
tions about the mechanisms underlying the results, including whether assignment to the
VSA negatively influenced study strategies or engagement with other learning resources.

Heterogeneity
6.1
By Student Characteristics
We first explore heterogeneity by student characteristics. First-generation status is the
most consistent dimension of differential treatment effects. In the full sample (Table 4),
the effect of VSA access on final grades was 2.87 points more negative (0.28 SDs) for first-
generation students than for their peers. The implied treatment effects were −5.10 points
(−0.50 SDs) for first-generation students and −2.23 points (−0.22 SDs) for other students.
This differential was larger in the exact-match sample (Table A4), at −3.89 points (−0.35
SDs). First-generation students also experienced larger reductions in homework scores:
20

an additional 2.29 points (0.22 SDs) in the full sample and 3.82 points (0.34 SDs) in the
exact-match sample. Their additional reductions in ELMS page views were 310.75 views
(0.20 SDs) and 440.81 views (0.22 SDs), respectively. These patterns suggest more pro-
nounced adverse academic effects among first-generation students, although statistical
precision varies across outcomes and samples.
There is little evidence that treatment effects differed systematically by prior academic
achievement. None of the interactions with high school GPA quartiles is statistically sig-
nificant in either sample, and the estimates show no clear achievement gradient. Gender
differences are also generally imprecisely estimated. The exception is an additional re-
duction of 8.77 recorded ELMS participations (0.30 SDs) among women relative to men
in the exact-match sample; this difference is only weakly significant (p<0.1) and is not
statistically significant in the full sample.
Racial and ethnic heterogeneity is more apparent in platform engagement than in aca-
demic performance. Relative to White students, Hispanic students experienced addi-
tional reductions of 383.47 page views (0.25 SDs) in the full sample and 526.04 page views
(0.27 SDs) in the exact-match sample. Students in the “other race” category experienced
additional reductions of 6.35 active days (0.42 SDs) and 6.05 active days (0.43 SDs) in the
full sample and exact-match sample, respectively. However, there is no statistically sig-
nificant racial or ethnic heterogeneity in final grades, course letter grade, or homework
scores.
Overall, the clearest recurring pattern is greater academic harm among first-generation
students, alongside some subgroup differences in platform engagement. Isolated signif-
icant findings warrant caution given the number of interactions examined, and reduced
ELMS activity need not imply reduced learning effort if VSA substitutes for some plat-
form use.

By Course Discipline
As mentioned above, we group all the participating courses into three buckets of disci-

7
Mechanisms
7.1
VSA Access and Broader AI Use
The effects reported in Table 3, particularly the substantial reductions in ELMS engage-
ment, are striking given the low uptake of the VSA. As discussed in Section 2.3, students
had access to both university-supported and commercial AI tools during the study. As-
signment to VSA access may therefore have affected outcomes not only through use of the
VSA itself, but also by changing the broader environment for AI use in treatment class-
rooms. For example, introducing the VSA may have increased the perceived acceptability
of GenAI or encouraged students to use other AI tools. Descriptively, Table 2 shows that
in the exact-match sample, lower final grades were not confined to students who used the
VSA but were similar to those who did not use the tool. The similarity between treated
users and nonusers is consistent with the possibility that the mere presence of the VSA
within the ELMS course space changed the broader classroom AI environment, not solely
use of the VSA itself.
We first examine this possibility using the student survey. These results require cau-
tion for two reasons. First, the overall response rate was low, at 18%. Second, controlling
for randomization blocks, treatment-group students were 4.3 percentage points less likely
to respond than control-group students (p < 0.01). Consequently, comparisons among re-
spondents may reflect differential selection into the survey as well as differences induced
by treatment assignment.
Figure 5 presents the unadjusted distributions of responses to a survey item concern-
ing instructor encouragement of GenAI use. Similar shares of treatment- and control-
group respondents characterized their instructors as neutral toward GenAI use. How-
ever, treatment-group respondents were substantially more likely to report that their in-
structors encouraged GenAI use or embedded it in the course (41% versus 11%) and less
likely to report that GenAI use was prohibited or discouraged.
23

These descriptive patterns suggest that students in treatment classrooms perceived a
more permissive environment for GenAI use. Resources provided to treatment instruc-
tors, including written guides, video tutorials, and weekly summaries of students’ VSA
usage, may have contributed to this environment. However, the survey does not establish
whether instructors changed their practices, whether students increased their use of other
AI tools, or whether either pathway explains the outcome effects. Moreover, the survey
question concerns GenAI generally rather than the VSA specifically. The small share re-
porting embedded GenAI use therefore suggests limited perceived curricular integration,
but should not be interpreted as a direct measure of students’ awareness of VSA access.
We next examine the implications of scaling the assignment effects by treatment-induced
VSA uptake. Using randomized assignment to VSA access as an instrumental variable
(IV) for using the VSA at least once, we examine the same set of outcomes as discussed
in the ITT analysis. A causal interpretation of these estimates requires, among other as-
sumptions, that assignment affects outcomes only through actual VSA use. This exclusion
restriction would be violated if assignment also changed instructor practices or students’
use of alternative AI tools besides the VSA. We therefore present these estimates as a
diagnostic exercise rather than interpret them as established causal effects of VSA use.
Appendix Table A6 presents the results. Assignment induced VSA usage by approxi-
mately 15 percent of the students in treated classrooms in the full sample and 13 percent in
the exact-match sample. The corresponding first-stage F-statistics were 50.5 and 16.5. Al-
though uptake was limited, assignment generated a meaningful first-stage relationship.
The IV estimates preserve the direction of the assignment effects but are much larger in
magnitude. For example, the estimated effect on final grades is −20.08 points in the full
sample and −31.36 points in the exact-match sample, equivalent to approximately −1.99
and −2.83 control-group SDs, respectively.
These large magnitudes arise in part mechanically because the IV estimator scales the
reduced-form ITT by a relatively small increase in VSA use. Nevertheless, they under-
24

score the strong assumptions required to attribute the full assignment effects to VSA use
alone. Combined with the survey evidence of greater perceived instructor encourage-
ment, they motivate considering broader changes in the classroom AI environment as a
possible mechanism.
Overall, the evidence is consistent with our conjecture that the ITT effects might oper-
ate through broader AI use or instructor practices. Our experiment identifies the effect of
offering the VSA in a setting where other AI tools were already widely available, which
is central to interpreting naturalistic evaluations of educational AI. While the ubiquity
of alternative tools does not preclude causal evaluation of access interventions, it makes
identifying tool-specific mechanisms increasingly difficult.
7.2
Engagement with Other Learning Activities
We now explore how access to the VSA might directly reduce student learning through
substituting their interactions with other key components of learning—instructors, peers,
and learning materials. Results reported in Table 3 suggest that VSA access significantly
reduced student engagement with ELMS, especially their active participation (−0.9 con-
trol SDs). We supplement this analysis by using a few relevant survey items. As the
bottom figure in Figure 5 shows, treatment-group respondents were more likely to some-
what or strongly agree with the statement, “I asked the instructor fewer questions about
course content” (approximately 57% versus 44%).
Comparing VSA users and those who did not use it within treated classrooms, Fig-
ure A6 also points to substitution of using AI for human interaction. The two groups of
students reported the largest differences on two items: “I sent the instructor fewer emails
or [ELMS] messages” and “I asked the instructor fewer questions about course content.”
They otherwise reported mostly similar perceptions on other course activities.
25

7.3
Content of Student Chats with the VSA
To investigate how students used the VSA, we developed a framework to classify stu-
dent requests into five categories: (1) Direct answer, encompassing requests for facts, def-
initions, conceptual explanations, solutions to exercises, or generated text or code; (2)
Practice and test preparation, including requests for practice problems, quizzes, and study
guides, as well as students’ answers to and requests for solutions to VSA-generated prac-
tice questions; (3) Course navigation and logistics, including questions about the location
of course materials, assignment requirements, deadlines, exam coverage, and whether
particular topics had been covered; (4) Feedback on an attempt, in which students sup-
plied their own reasoning, calculations, writing, or code for evaluation; and (5) Other
or unclear, encompassing greetings, acknowledgments, experimentation, and interactions
whose purpose could not be determined from the available context. Each student turn
received a single label corresponding to its primary purpose.
Two researchers from our team independently coded a simple random sample of 300
student requests. We used 100 examples to develop the classification instructions and
reserved 200 for held-out evaluation. The two annotators achieve an agreement for 92%
of the cases, resulting in a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.853. For codes the two annotators did
not agree upon, a third team member adjudicated the category. AI coding achieved 86%
accuracy across 200 student turns, with a weighted F1 of 0.86 and a macro F1 of 0.73 across
five categories. More detailed performance metrics for each coding category are reported
in Appendix Table A7. After evaluating the GPT-based classifier against the held-out set,
we applied the fixed classifier to the full corpus, using GPT-4.1 mini through the OpenAI
API under UMD’s enterprise agreement with OpenAI.
Of the 3,460 requests students sent to the VSA, 73.8% were classified as direct-answer
requests. Although such requests do not necessarily indicate cognitive offloading, their
predominance suggests that students primarily used the VSA to obtain information, ex-
planations, or solutions.
Practice and test preparation, which may reflect more self-
26

directed engagement, was the second most common category and accounts for 11.3%
of requests. Course navigation and logistics, other or unclear interactions, and feedback
on an attempt accounted for 7.6%, 6.6%, and 0.7% of requests, respectively. Requests for
feedback on students’ own work were thus particularly rare. Overall, while it is difficult
to gauge student cognitive engagement with the VSA using our coding categories, the
results suggest that students see the VSA as a source of immediate information gathering
rather than as a partner in sustained, interactive learning.
8
Discussion
This experimental study investigated the impact of offering a course-integrated GenAI-
powered study assistant across multiple disciplines at a large U.S. public university. Over
the course of a semester of implementation, we find that access to this tool did not im-
prove academic performance; rather, we find that in some cases, students in the treatment
condition had lower final grades. While there was evidence of a statistically significant
difference in conditions in full-match sample, the exact-match sample had differences
of approximately 0.37 SDs, a difference of about 4 points on a 0-100% scale. The most
consistent findings, however, were behavioral: access substantially reduced ELMS page
views, active days, and recorded participation. These results extend the evaluation of
educational AI beyond performance on assisted tasks to how its introduction changes
college students’ learning behaviors and engagement with the surrounding learning en-
vironment in a naturalistic higher education context.
The behavioral findings merit particular attention. Fewer page views in the learning
management system could reflect more efficient information retrieval, which is one goal
of the tool’s design. However, the decline extended to recorded participation—including
assignment submission, discussion responses, and quizzes—which fell by approximately
0.9 SDs in both analytical samples. Meanwhile, as we found no statistically detectable
27

effects on the share of homework submitted or submitted on time, the sizable reduction
on total platform participation suggests that students may have lowered their broader
engagement with course activities while continuing to meet assignment requirements.
The accompanying decline in final grades in the exact-match sample may suggest that the
reduced engagement with other learning activities might outweigh potential efficiency
gains through the VSA.
Our further analysis of the usage patterns and VSA chats provides descriptive context
for these findings. Students’ queries to the VSA are primarily composed of direct-answer
requests (73.8% of interactions), whereas practice and test preparation (11.3%) and re-
quests for feedback on students’ own attempts (7.6%) were much less common. Usage
was also episodic and often concentrated around examinations. Together, these patterns
suggest that students commonly used the VSA for on-demand information, explanations,
and solutions rather than sustained engagement through attempting, receiving feedback,
and revising. Survey reports of fewer instructor contacts are also consistent with the sub-
stitution in help-seeking.
Our findings complement the small but growing evidence that AI assistance can un-
dermine learning under some conditions (Bastani et al., 2025; Strömberg et al., 2026),
while contrasting with gains from deliberately structured AI tutoring (Kestin et al., 2025;
Oreopoulos and Low, 2026). One relevant distinction is between technical and instruc-
tional integration. Although the VSA retrieved instructor-selected materials and was em-
bedded in the learning management system, instructors did not receive any prescribed
approaches for incorporating it into learning activities, and none of the treatment-group
instructor survey respondents reported integrating it into assignments.
At the same
time, while they had the flexibility to choose between direct instruction and tutoring
modes, most instructors used the default direct instruction mode. Thus, course ground-
ing through the RAG technology and convenient access may improve the relevance of AI
assistance, but it does not ensure productive use. The results might have been different
28

had instructors received more extensive training on how to effectively use the VSA in
their instruction.
The interpretation of our results must take the broader AI environment in the uni-
versity and society into account. Because other AI tools were already available during
the study, introducing the VSA could have affected perceived permission to use AI, in-
structor practices, or students’ use of alternative tools. While only approximately 15% of
treated students used the VSA, behavior differences between the treatment and control
classrooms were substantial. Thus, the intervention likely evaluates an impact of broader
AI use induced by introducing the VSA and not just the isolated effect of individual VSA
use.
There are also several limitations of the study. For example, using course grades rather
than independently administered assessments makes interpreting a learning effect chal-
lenging. In addition, behavior outcomes from interactions with the learning management
system only capture a fraction of learning activities, with many others unobservable to the
researchers. Despite within-course random assignment, sections may still differ in grad-
ing and opportunities for platform participation. That said, it is only through exploration
of the real-world deployment of these tools that we can hope to develop a clear-eyed
understanding of the myriad factors that might enhance, hinder, or obscure the effect of
these tools in the very context for which they were developed. As the first RCT that evalu-
ates the impact of offering AI assistance across many disciplines, the results here highlight
a consequential deployment risk: expanding access to AI assistance may change engage-
ment with established learning activities and hinder course performance. Future research
should investigate to what extent tool designs and instructional pedagogy can enable AI
to be a complement to, rather than a substitute for, existing opportunities for practice,
feedback, and human interaction.
