NBC 10 I-Team: Rhode Island speed camera contracts could violate state law

by TAMARA SACHARCZYK, NBC 10 NEWS

Wed, March 8, 2023 at 5:55 PM
Updated Fri, March 10, 2023 at 10:34 AM
3VIEW ALL PHOTOS
A file image of a speed camera.

(WJAR) — When you get nabbed by a speed camera, you get slapped with a $50 fine.

A fixed portion of that fine then goes to the vendor behind the cameras, but a closer look at Rhode Island's speed camera law throws the legality of that into question.

The state law allowing speed cameras in Rhode Island was passed in 2016.

It states contracts between cities and vendors: "Shall be based on the value of such equipment and related support services and shall not be based on a percentage of the revenue generated by the automated school-zone-speed-enforcement system.”

Tickets are the only revenue generated by the speed cameras, and contracts between cities and vendors reviewed by the NBC 10 I-Team show each vendor takes a fixed fee from each citation.

“I think it’s probably a way of really strictly construing the law,” House Minority Leader Mike Chippendale said. “It certainly does feel like they’re getting a chunk, which the legislature made it clear that that was not our intent.”

  • ON YOUR DIME: Private vendors make millions from speed camera citations in Rhode Island

Providence, Pawtucket, East Providence, and Central Falls all have speed cameras.

As the I-Team previously reported, vendors are making big bucks off their contracts with those cities.

East Providence and Pawtucket have a contract with Sensys Gatso Group, an international company based in the Netherlands.

A file image of a speed camera. (WJAR)

According to the contracts, Sensys Gatso cashes in on $2,500 per camera, per month, plus $7.30 for each notice of violation.

In Providence, New Jersey-based vendor Conduent gets a $2,978 fixed fee per camera per month and collects $7.85 per each notice of violation.

All speed camera citations have a set $50 fine.

We brought our findings to NBC 10 Legal Analyst Mark Dana.

“There’s no question that with this statute, if you were getting a percentage of the ticket or a fixed amount of the ticket, it’s a violation," he said.

Dana zeroed in on the “shall” and “shall not” language in the statute.

  • ON YOUR DIME: Rhode Island businesses not seeing big tax breaks in budget proposal

“The statute says they shall not use a percentage, so in Rhode Island, they are using a fixed amount that might be $7.50 of a $50 ticket, but that is a percentage, that is 15%,” Dana said. “The statute tells us that it must be based on the value of the equipment, that’s how much they should get paid, whatever the value is or the maintenance is, which they are entitled to be paid for. They cannot get any monies based on tickets.”

The cities with the cameras disagree.

Pawtucket sent us a statement, arguing the city pays that fixed fee even if the citation is dismissed and therefore, it's not a percentage of the profit.

"The contract the city has with Sensys Gatso, the vendor, states that Sensys Gatso receives $15.40 for each notice of violation issued for red-light cameras and $7.30 for each notice of violation issued for speed enforcement cameras. These fixed amounts are neither based on nor are they a percentage of fines. A violation approved by the Pawtucket Police Department might later be dismissed or adjudicated as not guilty, but the City would still be obligated to pay Sensys Gatso even though the City would not collect a fine associated with that violation. As a result, it is the opinion of the Administration that a fixed fee assessed for the processing and mailing of each City approved notice of violation, is not in violation of the statute."

Providence sent us a different explanation for why the contract isn’t violating the law, stating that the fixed fee addresses the administrative costs of tickets and therefore is ok.

“Payment based on revenue is often included in an agreement as a percentage of overall revenue and not a fixed fee like the $7.30 fee. That fixed fee exists in the contract to address the administrative cost of tickets,” a city spokesperson said.

While the cities believe they’re in their legal right, not everyone is so sure.

“On its face, it does seem to be violating what the General Assembly was trying to prevent in 2016 when they authorized these systems,” Common Cause R.I. Executive Director John Marion said.

However, proving the intent of a law that passed years ago may be difficult to prove.

  • ON YOUR DIME : Legal battle over Woonsocket mayor's ouster cost taxpayers $150K

“The legislature doesn’t really produce a record like Congress does,” Marion said. “In Congress, most bills are accompanied by a report that sort of explains in regular language what the law is trying to do. Rhode Island doesn’t do that, so it’s a lot harder to get the intent of what a law was.”

Since the vendors behind the cameras operate in numerous states, and in some cases, numerous countries, Marion says it’s possible they provide standard contracts to cities and towns. “I would assume for big multinational companies, a boilerplate contract is pretty common and might not have been vetted fully,” he said.

Providence initiated speed cameras before the state law passed in 2016, but a city spokesperson says the previous administration amended the contract to be in compliance with the new legislation.

“There is some language in the Providence contract that spells out that the city gets a certain amount per ticket, but then there’s the last clause that says or a fixed fee as required by law,” Marion said. “They may have been anticipating that the state was going to prohibit the per ticket payment and decided that the contract should reflect that there would be a different way of compensating the contractor.”

That clause in Providence’s contract is missing from the contracts in East Providence and Pawtucket.

Marion says if there’s a chance the law is being violated, the legislature has an oversight role.

“This is sort of a natural topic when your reporting exposes something like this for the legislature and its oversight committees in the House and Senate to take a look,” Marion said. “Anytime they pass a statute, they can look and see if that statute is being enforced properly.”

Chippendale believes the entire statute needs to be revisited.

“I do think we should look at the statute again to see if there’s some tightening of language that can be done to make sure our taxpayers, and our motorists in the state, aren’t paying too much and that this revenue is not being generated for a private company to ensure safety around children,” he said. “We have heard stories of tickets still being given after-hours. There’s just something that doesn’t sit well with my constituents or constituents with the state that a computer is handing them a ticket essentially.”
