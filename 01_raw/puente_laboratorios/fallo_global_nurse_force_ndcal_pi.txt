I. 
BACKGROUND 
It is important at the outset for the Court to make clear that this case is not about whether 
the challenged Proclamation is good or bad, or the appropriate policy response to the concerns it 
describes.  These policy questions are the subject of extensive, and often intense, differences of 
opinion, and this Court cannot—and does not—express any view as to them.  The Court considers 
only the legal questions presented here regarding whether the Proclamation and resulting agency 
policies exceed the Executive Branch’s constitutional and statutory authority. 
The Court also acknowledges that it does not decide this motion—or consider this case—in 
a vacuum.  Two other lawsuits have challenged the Proclamation and reached different 
conclusions.  In Chamber of Commerce v. United States Department of Homeland Security, the 
district court for the District of Columbia upheld the Proclamation and its $100,000 fee for H-1B 
petitions, finding that the President acted within his statutory authority under the Immigration and 
Nationality Act (“INA”) and that the agency’s policies implementing the fee did not violate the 
Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”).  815 F. Supp. 3d 73, 79 (D.D.C. 2025).  The case is 
currently on appeal in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit. 
In California v. Mullin, the district court for the District of Massachusetts recently declared 
unlawful and vacated the Department of Homeland Security’s and the Department of State’s 
policies implementing the $100,000 fee.  833 F. Supp. 3d 50, 60–84 (D. Mass. 2026).  The First 
Circuit later denied the government’s request for a stay pending appeal.  See California v. Mullin, 
183 F.4th 42, 46–52 (1st Cir. 2026) (“Mullin II”).  The agency policies discussed further below are 
thus currently vacated.  This Court is not, however, precluded from considering Plaintiffs’ motion 
and requested relief as the parties in both Chamber of Commerce and Mullin are pursuing further 
judicial review.  Cf. Chafin v. Chafin, 568 U.S. 165, 172 (2013) (“As long as the parties have a

A. 
The H-1B Visa Program  
The Immigration and Nationality Act (“INA”) governs the admission of noncitizens into 
the United States.  See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.  To enter the United States, noncitizens must 
generally present a valid visa or other travel document, such as a passport, and be found 
admissible upon inspection by an immigration officer at a port of entry.  See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1181, 
1182(a)(7)(A)(i) and (B)(i), 1185(d), 1201(h), 1225(a); cf. Trump v. Hawaii, 585 U.S. 667, 694 
(2018) (recognizing “the basic distinction between admissibility determinations and visa 
issuance”). 
In 1990 Congress established the H-1B visa program, which provides a pathway for 
American employers to hire skilled, nonimmigrant foreign workers in certain “specialty 
occupations.”4  See 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(H)(i)(B); 8 U.S.C. § 1184(i).  H-1B  visas fall into two

B. 
Presidential Proclamation 10973  
On September 19, 2025, the President signed Proclamation 10973, which requires a 
supplemental payment of $100,000 for all H-1B petitions.6  See 90 Fed. Reg. 46,027 (Sept. 19, 
2025).  It became effective two days later, on September 21, 2025, and based on the recent 
extension it will not expire until September 21, 2027.  See id. at 46,028; see also 91 Fed. Reg.

perform services in a specialty occupation [under the H-1B visa program] is restricted, except for 
those aliens whose petitions are accompanied or supplemented by a payment of $100,000 . . . .”  
Id. (Section 1(a)).  The Proclamation accordingly directs the Secretary of Homeland Security to 
“restrict decisions on petitions not accompanied by a $100,000 payment for H-1B specialty

C. 
Implementing the Proclamation 
On September 20, 2025, the day after the President signed the Proclamation, USCIS issued 
a memorandum clarifying that the Proclamation “only applies prospectively to petitions that have 
not yet been filed.”  See Dkt. No. 46-5, Ex. 4 (“USCIS Memo”) (emphasis omitted).  The 
memorandum states that the Proclamation thus “does not apply to aliens who:  are the 
beneficiaries of petitions that were filed prior to the effective date of the proclamation, are the 
beneficiaries of currently approved petitions, or are in possession of validly issued H-1B non-
immigrant visas.”  Id.  That same day, CBP in turn issued a memorandum similarly explaining that 
the Proclamation is prospective, and explaining that USCIS and the Department of State were 
“instructed to begin implementing the new monetary requirements for employers submitting 
petitions on behalf of aliens outside the United States for new H-1B petitions only.”  See Dkt. No. 
46-6, Ex. 5 (“CBP Memo”).  The CBP Memo stated that the Proclamation “does not impact the 
ability of any current visa holder to travel to or from the United States.”  Id.   
On September 21, 2025, the day the Proclamation took effect, both USCIS and the 
Department of State released “FAQs” regarding the Proclamation.  See Dkt. No. 46-7, Ex. 6 
(“USCIS FAQ”); Dkt. No. 46-8, Ex. 7 (“State Dept. FAQ”).  Like the earlier memorandums, the 
FAQs reiterated that the Proclamation required a $100,000 payment for “new” visa petitions as of 
September 21, 2026, but did not “[a]pply to any previously issued H-1B visas.”  Id.  The FAQs 
further stated that the payment did not apply to “any H-1B renewals.”  See id.  This guidance, 
however, did not explain how to make the payment or how to seek a national interest exception.

III. 
LEGAL STANDARD 
A. 
Motion for Preliminary Injunction 
Plaintiffs seeking preliminary relief must establish that:  (1) they are likely to succeed on 
the merits; (2) they are likely to suffer irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary relief; (3) the 
balance of equities tips in their favor; and (4) an injunction is in the public interest.  Winter v. Nat. 
Res. Def. Council, 555 U.S. 7, 20 (2008).  Preliminary relief is “an extraordinary remedy that may 
only be awarded upon a clear showing that the plaintiff is entitled to such relief.”  Id. at 22.  
Where, as here, the government is a party, the public interest and the balance of equities factors 
merge.  Drakes Bay Oyster Co. v. Jewell, 747 F.3d 1073, 1092 (9th Cir. 2014) (citing Nken v. 
Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 435 (2009)).   
A court must find that “a certain threshold showing” is made on each of the four required 
elements.  Leiva-Perez v. Holder, 640 F.3d 962, 966 (9th Cir. 2011).  Under the Ninth Circuit’s 
sliding scale approach, a preliminary injunction may issue if there are “serious questions going to 
the merits” if “a hardship balance [also] tips sharply towards the [movant],” and “so long as the 
[movant] also shows that there is a likelihood of irreparable injury and that the injunction is in the 
public interest.”  All. for the Wild Rockies v. Cottrell, 632 F.3d 1127, 1135 (9th Cir. 2011). 
Under either standard, Plaintiffs bear the burden of making a clear showing that they are 
entitled to this extraordinary remedy.  Earth Island Inst. v. Carlton, 626 F.3d 462, 469 (9th Cir. 
2010).  “Likelihood of success on the merits is the most important Winter factor.”  Disney Enters., 
Inc. v. VidAngel, Inc., 869 F.3d 848, 856 (9th Cir. 2017) (quotation omitted).

IV. 
DISCUSSION 
A. 
Standing  
As an initial matter, Defendants contend that Plaintiffs lack Article III standing to 
challenge the Proclamation and Agency Policies.  Dkt. No. 98 at 11–15; see also Dkt. No. 104 at 
3–8.  Article III of the Constitution “confines the federal judicial power to the resolution of 
‘Cases’ and ‘Controversies.’”  TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 594 U.S. 413, 423 (2021).  “For there 
to be a case or controversy under Article III, the plaintiff must have a personal stake in the case—
in other words, standing.”  Id. (quotation omitted).  Specifically, to meet its burden of establishing 
standing, a plaintiff must show it has (i) “suffered an injury in fact that is concrete, particularized, 
and actual or imminent; (ii) that the injury was likely caused by the defendant; and (iii) that the 
injury would likely be redressed by judicial relief.”  Id. (citing Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 
U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992)).  “At the preliminary injunction stage, [] the plaintiff must make a ‘clear 
showing’ that [it] is ‘likely’ to establish each element of standing.”  Murthy v. Missouri, 603 U.S. 
43, 58 (2024) (quoting Winter, 555 U.S. at 22). 
Here, Defendants urge that Plaintiffs’ “injuries rest on a ‘speculative chain of possibilities’ 
that are not clearly traceable to the Proclamation.”  See Dkt. No. 98 at 13 (quoting Murthy, 603 
U.S. at 70).  The Court is not persuaded.9

v. Bell, 430 U.S. 787, 796 (1977) (noting that “[t]he conditions of entry for every alien . . . have

power of this Court to control”).  As the Supreme Court has noted, when “Congress [] delegate[s]

However, Defendants’ argument misses the mark.  Plaintiffs are not challenging the 
manner in which Defendants exercised their discretionary authority to admit or deny noncitizens.

Defendants’ cited cases suggest that the consular nonreviewability doctrine applies to shield 
purported ultra vires conduct from judicial review.  Muñoz, for example, involved a challenge to 
an individual visa denial.  602 U.S. at 902–18.  The respondent—a U.S. citizen—brought a Due 
Process challenge to the consular officer’s denial of her noncitizen husband’s visa application.  Id. 
at 902.  The Court noted that consular nonreviewability barred it from considering any challenge 
by the noncitizen husband to the consular officer’s decision.  See id. at 908–09.  Nevertheless, the 
Court considered the respondent’s constitutional challenge under “a narrow exception” to the 
doctrine.  Id. at 909–19 (holding that U.S. citizens do not have a fundamental liberty interest in the 
admission of a noncitizen spouse).  Similarly, in Knauff v. Shaughnessy, the petitioner was 
challenging the Attorney General’s order to exclude her without a hearing for security reasons.

The Ninth Circuit, on the other hand, has rejected the argument that executive policies and 
practices, even in the immigration context, are completely unreviewable.  See Washington v. 
Trump, 847 F.3d 1151, 1161–62 (9th Cir. 2017).  In Washington, the plaintiffs challenged an 
executive order which “ban[ned] for 90 days the entry into the United States of individuals from 
seven countries.”  Id. at 1156.  The government argued that the executive order was unreviewable, 
relying at least in part on the doctrine of consular nonreviewability.  Id. at 1161–63 (citing 
Kleindienst v. Mandel, 408 U.S. 753 (1972)).  In rejecting this argument, the court emphasized the 
critical distinction between “the President’s promulgation of a sweeping immigration policy” and 
“an executive branch official’s decision to issue or deny an individual visa.”  Id. (emphasis in 
original); cf. Patel v. Reno, 134 F.3d 929, 932 (9th Cir. 1997) (considering merits of petitioner’s 
case where they were “challenging the consul’s authority to suspend their visa applications, not 
challenging a decision within the discretion of the consul”).  “[E]xercises of policymaking 
authority at the highest levels of the political branches are plainly not subject to [the doctrine of 
consular nonreviewability].”  Washington, 847 F.3d at 1162.  To the contrary, “it is beyond 
question that the federal judiciary retains the authority to adjudicate constitutional challenges to 
executive action.”  Id. at 1164; see also id. at 1163 (“This is no less true when the challenged 
immigration action implicates national security concerns.”).

a. Claims against Agency Defendants 
Plaintiffs bring several claims against Agency Defendants under the APA.  The APA 
requires courts to “hold unlawful and set aside agency action . . . found to be arbitrary, capricious, 
an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.”  5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A).  
Defendants attempt to shield Agency Defendants from scrutiny, urging that the Agency Policies 
are not distinct from the Proclamation itself, and thus are unreviewable.  See Dkt. No. 98 at 20–22, 
39–40.  Relatedly, they argue that there is no final agency action for purposes of APA review 
because the Agency Defendants were bound to follow the Proclamation.  Id. at 22–25.  The Court 
disagrees. 
Despite Defendants’ efforts to distinguish the case, the Court finds the Ninth Circuit’s 
opinion in Nebraska v. Su directly on point.  121 F.4th 1 (9th Cir. 2024).  In Su, the plaintiffs 
challenged President Biden’s Executive Order 14026, which required federal contractors to pay 
employees a $15 minimum wage.  Id. at 5–6.  In response, and after notice and comment, the DOL 
issued a final rule implementing the executive order.  See id.  The plaintiffs sought to challenge 
both the executive order and the DOL’s implementing rule.  Id. at 6.  The court reviewed both the 
executive order and the DOL’s implementing rule, finding that they exceeded the statutory 
authority Congress provided under the Federal Property and Administrative Services Act 
(“FPASA”), and that the DOL, in issuing its implementing rule, had violated the APA.15 
The DOL argued that the APA did not apply to the DOL’s rule that implemented the 
executive order.  See id. at 14.  In rejecting this argument, the Ninth Circuit pointed out that “[n]o 
language in the APA prevents or excepts review of an agency action that implements a

Under the APA, when promulgating a “legislative rule,” an agency must publish a 
proposed rule and give the public “an opportunity to participate in the rule making through 
submission of written data, views, or arguments . . . .”  5 U.S.C. § 553(b)–(c).  Only after 
considering relevant materials can the agency finalize the rule.  See 5 U.S.C. § 553(c).  By its 
terms, § 553 does not apply to “interpretative rules.”  See 5 U.S.C. § 553(b)(A).  “[I]nterpretive 
rules merely explain, but do not add to, the substantive law that already exists in the form of a 
statute or legislative rule.”  Hemp Indus. Ass’n v. DEA, 333 F.3d 1082, 1087 (9th Cir. 2003).  
“Legislative rules, on the other hand, create rights, impose obligations, or effect a change in 
existing law pursuant to authority delegated by Congress.”  Id.  In other words, a legislative rule 
has the “force of law.”  Id. (quotation omitted).  A rule may have the “force of law” “(1) when, in 
the absence of the rule, there would not be an adequate legislative basis for enforcement action; 
(2) when the agency has explicitly invoked its general legislative authority; or (3) when the rule 
effectively amends a prior legislative rule.”  Id. 
Here, Defendants concede that Agency Defendants did not conduct any notice and

See Dkt. No. 98 at 37 (describing notice-and-comment rulemaking as “meaningless” in the face of 
the Proclamation).  Instead, Defendants urge that the Agency Policies are not “legislative rules”

But the Ninth Circuit, in contrast, has held that “[t]he public interest is served by 
compliance with the APA,” and “it does not matter that notice and comment could have changed 
the substantive result; the public interest is served from the proper process itself.”  East Bay 
Sanctuary, 993 F.3d at 678 (quoting California v. Azar, 911 F.3d 558, 581 (9th Cir. 2018)).  
Accordingly, the Ninth Circuit has stated that “[t]he court must exercise great caution in applying 
the harmless error rule in the administrative rulemaking context.”  Azar, 911 F.3d at 580 
(quotation omitted).  “The failure to provide notice and comment is harmless only where the 
agency’s mistake clearly had no bearing on the procedure used or the substance of decision 
reached.”  Id. (cleaned up).  The record before the Court does not support such a conclusion. 
ii. 
Likelihood of Irreparable Injury  
A plaintiff seeking a preliminary injunction must “demonstrate that irreparable injury is 
likely in the absence of an injunction.”  Winter, 555 U.S. at 22.   
Defendants argue that the harm to Plaintiffs is purely economic and thus not irreparable 
because “the Court (in theory) could order the one-time payments returned.”  See Dkt. No. 98 at 
41–42.  But Defendants ignore Plaintiffs’ compelling evidence that they cannot afford to pay the 
fee in the first place.  BAE Industries, for example, could not afford the $100,000 payment for its 
valued Senior Quality Engineer.  See Zylka Decl. at ¶ 11.  Without this engineer, BAE Industries 
explained that it would “lose the years of knowledge, experience, and trust that this employee has 
developed.”  Id. at ¶¶ 12, 14–15.  Nephrology Associates, likewise, has suffered a “reduced ability 
to provide medical services to the local population,” as well as “disruption of business operations, 
and increased workload for existing physicians” because of its inability to pay the $100,000 fee.  
See Waters Decl. at ¶¶ 6–11, 20; see also Witte Decl. at ¶¶ 4–5 (describing need for additional 
teachers but all applicants will require H-1B visa sponsorship); Pattanaik Decl. at ¶¶ 14–15 
(describing loss of clients and existential threat to business because of fee).  The Court finds that 
Plaintiffs have shown a likelihood of irreparable harm absent the requested injunction.

V. 
REQUEST FOR BOND AND STAY PENDING APPEAL 
Defendants request that “to the extent the Court is inclined to issue a universal preliminary 
injunction, it should require bond.”  Dkt. No. 98 at 44–45.  Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 
65(c), “[t]he court may issue a . . . temporary restraining order only if the movant gives security in 
an amount that the court considers proper to pay the costs and damages sustained by any party 
found to have been wrongfully enjoined or restrained.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 65(c).  However, district 
courts have considerable discretion in determining whether the posting of a bond is necessary: 
 
Despite the seemingly mandatory language, Rule 65(c) invests the 
district court with discretion as to the amount of security required, if 
any. In particular, the district court may dispense with the filing of a 
bond when it concludes there is no realistic likelihood of harm to the 
defendant from enjoining his or her conduct. 
Johnson v. Couturier, 572 F.3d 1067, 1086 (9th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted).  Defendants make 
little effort to explain the likelihood of harm that would occur in light of an injunction.  See Dkt. 
No. 98 at 44–45.  They suggest that domestic workers will be “replace[d] by foreign labor” and 
that posting a bond will not impose a financial hardship given Plaintiffs’ extensive assets.  Id.  But 
the Court is simply not persuaded that a bond is necessary where, as here, the Court found that 
Plaintiffs have shown a likelihood of success on the merits as to the claim that the Agency 
Defendants failed to comply with the APA’s requirements.  The Court exercises its discretion and 
waives any bond requirement under the circumstances.

VI. 
CONCLUSION 
The Court GRANTS IN PART and DENIES IN PART Plaintiffs’ motion for preliminary 
injunction as discussed above.  Dkt. No. 75.  The Agency Policies are VACATED and 
REMANDED to Agency Defendants.  Agency Defendants, and all persons acting under their 
direction, are enjoined from enforcing or implementing the Agency Policies made pursuant to or 
in implementation of Proclamation No. 10973 and its extension in Proclamation No. 11069, until 
notice-and-comment rulemaking under the APA and regulatory flexibility analyses under the RFA 
have been conducted. 
The Court DENIES the motion to dismiss.  Dkt. No. 104.  Defendants raise overlapping 
arguments in their opposition to the motion for preliminary injunction, but as the complexity of 
the issues discussed above suggests, this is not a case well-suited to a decision at the motion to 
dismiss stage.  The Court further notes that the legal landscape has shifted considerably since 
Defendants first filed the motion to dismiss, and further briefing may be needed to squarely 
address any remaining issues. 
The Court DENIES the related provisional motion to certify the class without prejudice.  
Dkt. No. 76.  Even assuming the requirements of Rule 23(a) and Rule 23(b)(2) are met, it is not 
clear to the Court why class certification is necessary under the current circumstances.  
Certification is not required for the Court to enter the requested preliminary injunction, which it 
has done.  Putative Class Members suggested that certification would “ensure[] that any 
declaratory and injunctive relief [would] apply consistently and fairly to all those affected . . . .”  
Dkt. No. 102 at 1.  But Plaintiffs and any putative class members are now doubly protected:  the 
Mullin court already vacated the Agency Policies such that the $100,000 fee is no longer in effect, 
and the First Circuit declined to stay the vacatur pending appeal.  To the extent the Putative Class 
Plaintiffs believe class treatment is nonetheless still necessary, they may renew their motion for 
class certification and update their motion in light of current circumstances.
